Research

I am interested in various problems in algebraic and geometric topology, particularly at the interface between cohomology theory, low-dimensional topology, and mathematical physics. Below I describe the directions I have been pursuing so far, and where my current interests are heading.

Cohomology of Homogeneous Spaces

Cohomology is one of the main tools algebraic topologists use to distinguish spaces that look different but share the same basic shape invariants. A particularly rich family of examples comes from homogeneous spaces such as Stiefel manifolds and complex Grassmannians, which parametrize collections of orthonormal frames or subspaces in a vector space and appear throughout geometry, representation theory, and physics.

In my Master's thesis (Université of Yaoundé I, supervised by Dr. Hilaire George Mbiakop), I studied the cohomology of these spaces using the Gysin exact sequence, together with Euler classes and Chern classes, which encode how a vector bundle twists over its base space. This work sits at the intersection of algebraic topology and differential geometry, and it shaped my interest in how algebraic invariants can be used to compute and organize topological information.

Low-Dimensional Topology and Knot Theory

A central question in low-dimensional topology is understanding how a knot sits inside 3-dimensional space, and what the complement of that knot — its exterior — can tell us about the knot itself. A knot exterior is fibered if it can be built by taking a surface and gluing its boundary back to itself via a "twisting" map, layer by layer, like pages of a book curling around a spine.

In my MSc thesis at AIMS Rwanda (supervised by Prof. Ana García Lecuona, University of Glasgow), I studied Stallings' Fibration Theorem, which gives an algebraic criterion — in terms of the knot group and the Alexander polynomial — for deciding exactly when a knot exterior fibers over the circle. This theorem sits at a beautiful crossroad between algebra (group theory), topology (3-manifolds), and combinatorics (polynomial invariants), and it continues to be my main entry point into knot theory, fibered 3-manifolds, and more broadly contact and symplectic topology.

Mathematical Physics: Supergravity and Geometry

Supergravity theories describe gravity together with supersymmetry, and many of their solutions are built from highly structured geometric spaces — Kähler manifolds, Riemannian and Lorentzian geometries — whose curvature and holonomy encode the physical fields.

As part of the MIORPA programme at the University of Oxford (selected among twelve participants, working with Tabea Sieper and Chris Couzens), I have been studying supergravity solutions built from toric and Kähler geometry, using Mathematica for symbolic computation. This work has given me a concrete appreciation for how differential-geometric machinery — curvature, complex structures, moment maps — gets put to work in a physical setting, and it feeds back naturally into my interest in geometric and symplectic topology.

Topological Data Analysis and Stochastic Geometry

More recently I have become interested in topological data analysis (TDA), which uses tools from algebraic topology (persistent homology, simplicial complexes) to extract robust shape information from noisy data, and in stochastic geometry, which studies random geometric structures such as random point processes, tessellations, and their limiting behaviour. My coursework at AIMS Rwanda included a dedicated TDA course as well as substantial training in probability and stochastic processes, and I am keen to explore probabilistic approaches to TDA as a bridge between my topological background and applied/data-driven questions.
